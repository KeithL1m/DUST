# ☕ Morning Coffee — September 2, 2026

The Iran war is the gravitational center of today's news: it's now driving Federal Reserve policy, pulling Russia and China closer to Tehran at a summit in Bishkek, and rattling oil shipping lanes the world's economy still depends on. Meanwhile, Washington's approval numbers are sinking and London's brand-new PM is making his opening pitch to a still-jittery bond market.

---

## US Strikes Iran Again as Hormuz Tanker Attacks Escalate

**Two supertankers were hit by unidentified projectiles off Oman on August 31, killing two sailors, and the US answered September 1–2 with fresh strikes on Iranian air defenses and radar sites — the ceasefire from June is effectively dead.**

The Sidr and the Senegal Prosperity, each carrying roughly 2 million barrels of crude, were struck near Khasab, Oman, killing two Filipino sailors. The US retaliated on September 1–2 against Iranian air defenses and radar — Trump said "we took out all of the new equipment that they tried to build along the Strait of Hormuz." Iran reported at least 15 casualties, and the rial hit a record low.

At a Shanghai Cooperation Organization summit in Bishkek, Russia's Putin told Iranian President Pezeshkian "we are supplying you with the necessary goods." Not everyone backed that framing — India's Modi told the summit "every day of war sets humanity back a step," a notable public break from the Russia-Iran line.

**Why it matters:** Hormuz carried roughly a fifth of the world's oil before the war, and each strike cycle makes normal transit less likely soon. Bishkek shows Iran has diplomatic cover despite US sanctions, though Modi's dissent suggests that bloc is less unified than Moscow projects. Watch whether the strike cycle keeps this pace — it's the direct channel by which this war is reaching energy markets and the Fed's rate call below.

## Markets Now Expect the Fed to Hike Rates in September

**Futures markets are pricing roughly 65% odds of a quarter-point Fed rate increase this month — a sharp reversal from a "cuts" outlook earlier this year — driven by Iran-war energy shocks and doubts about the Fed's inflation resolve.**

At its July meeting the Fed held its rate at 3.50%–3.75% on a 9-3 vote, with three members dissenting for an immediate hike. Since then energy costs have stayed elevated and the 30-year Treasury yield hit its highest level since 2007. J.P. Morgan's Phil Camporeale: "the combination of slower-than-expected supply chain normalization and market questioning of inflation-fighting credibility has lowered the bar for a rate hike."

This reverses 2025's posture, when the Fed cut rates three straight meetings — one of the clearest cases yet of a foreign conflict overriding a central bank's domestic policy path.

**Why it matters:** A hike raises borrowing costs just as affordability concerns already weigh on sentiment — and on Trump's approval numbers, below. Watch the next two CPI prints and any further Gulf escalation, both flagged as deciding factors.

## Ukraine's Parliament Blocks Reform Bill, Putting €3.7B in EU Aid at Risk

**Ukraine's parliament fell 30 votes short of passing a law eliminating tax exemptions on small postal packages on September 1 — one of 12 conditions tied to €3.7 billion in EU Macro-Financial Assistance the European Commission says is now at risk.**

The bill is one of 44 reforms due by November 1; more are up for debate September 2–3. Zelensky addressed the delay directly: "they may be difficult decisions... but they are necessary."

This isn't a one-off — Ukraine already missed €3.7 billion in EU support in 2025 under the same mechanism, and the Commission warned August 25 that rule-of-law progress remains "vital" for membership.

**Why it matters:** The repeat pattern signals a structural risk to Ukraine's financing, not a one-off fight, and complicates EU accession just as Western attention is split by the Iran war. Watch the September 2–3 votes for whether parliament reverses course before November 1.

## Trump's Approval Ties Career Low at 33% as Army Secretary Quits Amid Pentagon Feud

**Trump's approval rating has settled at 33% in the latest Reuters/Ipsos survey — tying the lowest point of his political career — while Army Secretary Dan Driscoll resigned Monday after months of friction with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth.**

The 33% figure is a 14-point drop from the 47% Trump held at the start of his second term; 71% disapprove of his cost-of-living handling. Driscoll's exit followed Hegseth ousting Gen. Randy George, a close Driscoll ally, as Army Chief of Staff in April.

Both land against a midterm backdrop where election administration is contested too — Tarrant County, Texas approved cutting polling locations by 92 versus 2022. Deputy Army Undersecretary Dave Fitzgerald is also expected to step down this week.

**Why it matters:** Approval stuck in the low-to-mid 30s heading into a midterm year has historically preceded major losses for the incumbent's party, and a visible Pentagon fight adds to that picture. Watch whether Fitzgerald's exit is followed by more departures.

## UK's New PM Burnham Sets Out Devolution-Focused Opening Agenda

**Prime Minister Andy Burnham gave his first statement to the House of Commons on September 1, centering his agenda on devolving power to England's regions and cutting costs on energy bills, pub rates, and bus fares — with a full fiscal plan due in October.**

Burnham announced "Number 10 North" and extended devolution offers to every English area without existing devolved powers. Near-term measures include a VAT cut on electricity bills and reinstating the £2 bus fare cap through 2027.

Starmer had warned before resigning that Burnham's instincts risked a Liz Truss-style market reaction; UK gilt yields have since steadied.

**Why it matters:** October's budget is the real test of whether Burnham's agenda is fiscally credible to markets already jittery during his rise to power. His early moves suggest banking visible wins before that harder reckoning arrives.

## China Steps Up Preparations for a Taiwan Blockade, Taipei Warns

**Taiwan's defense ministry says the PLA is intensifying preparations for a blockade of the island, according to its 2026 China Military Power Report submitted to the legislature this week alongside a five-year force development plan and defense budget request.**

The ministry says PLA exercises increasingly focus on "joint blockade and control" of Taiwan's air and sea routes. Taiwan says it's developing countermeasures, tested for the first time during an August war-games exercise.

The report lands the same week Xi Jinping appeared alongside Russia and Iran at the Bishkek summit, underscoring Beijing positioning itself within a bloc contesting US-led pressure campaigns.

**Why it matters:** A shift in PLA training toward blockade scenarios is a leading indicator analysts watch for contingency planning, not routine posturing. Watch Taiwan's actual defense budget figures when finalized.

### 🔍 The Big Picture

Nearly every story today traces back to the same fault line: the Iran war has stopped being a regional conflict and become the organizing event for global politics and economics. It's forcing the Fed's hand on rates, pushing Russia, Iran, and China into closer alignment at forums like the SCO even as India visibly resists that framing, and testing whether Gulf shipping lanes can keep functioning under repeated strikes.

The thread connecting Washington's sinking approval numbers, Beijing's Taiwan posture, and the Bishkek summit is a world where US pressure campaigns are increasingly met with coordinated pushback, even if that pushback isn't as unified as its participants project. Watch whether the September Fed decision and the next Hormuz escalation cycle move in tandem — that link will define how contained this period turns out to be.
