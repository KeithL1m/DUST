# ☕ Morning Coffee — September 2, 2026

The Iran war is the gravitational center of today's news: it's now driving Federal Reserve policy, pulling Russia and China closer to Tehran at a summit in Bishkek, and rattling oil shipping lanes the world's economy still depends on. Meanwhile, two of the West's governments are mid-transition and finding it rocky — Washington's approval numbers are sinking as a defense-department feud spills into public view, and London's brand-new prime minister is making his opening pitch to a skeptical Parliament and a still-jittery bond market.

## US Strikes Iran Again as Hormuz Tanker Attacks Escalate

**Two supertankers were hit by unidentified projectiles off Oman on August 31, killing two sailors, and the US answered September 1–2 with fresh strikes on Iranian air defenses and radar sites — the ceasefire from June is effectively dead.**

The Sidr and the Senegal Prosperity, each carrying roughly 2 million barrels of crude, were struck minutes apart near Khasab, Oman, while transiting the US-guarded southern corridor of the Strait of Hormuz on August 31, killing two Filipino sailors. The US responded on September 1–2 with strikes on Iranian air defenses, radar, and mine-laying capability — Trump said "we took out all of the new equipment that they tried to build along the Strait of Hormuz." Iran reported at least 15 casualties, and the rial hit a record low against the euro. The June ceasefire memorandum collapsed after Iran resumed targeting vessels it said were bypassing an approved transit route, and Secretary of State Rubio has ruled out reviving its old terms.

The fallout is pulling in other powers. At a Shanghai Cooperation Organization summit in Bishkek on September 1, Russia's Putin told Iranian President Pezeshkian "we are supplying you with the necessary goods," while Pezeshkian urged the bloc to help Iran "bypass unilateral measures." Not everyone at the table backed that framing — India's Modi told the summit "every day of war sets humanity back a step; we need to move from endless war to its conclusion," a notable public break from the Russia-Iran line.

**Why it matters:** Shipping traffic through Hormuz — a corridor that carried roughly a fifth of the world's oil before the war — has cratered, and each strike-and-retaliation cycle makes a return to normal transit less likely soon. Bishkek shows Iran has diplomatic cover even under intensified US sanctions, and Modi's public dissent suggests that "anti-US bloc" framing is less unified than Moscow and Tehran project. Watch whether the tanker-strike cycle keeps this pace — it's the direct channel by which this war is reaching global energy markets and, by extension, the Fed's rate decision below.

## Markets Now Expect the Fed to Hike Rates in September

**Futures markets are pricing roughly 65% odds of a quarter-point Fed rate increase this month — a sharp reversal from a "cuts" outlook earlier this year — driven by Iran-war energy shocks and doubts about the Fed's inflation resolve.**

At its July meeting the Fed held its rate at 3.50%–3.75% on a 9-3 vote, with three members dissenting in favor of an immediate hike — a split that itself unsettled markets. Since then, Middle East supply-chain disruption tied to the Iran conflict has kept energy costs elevated, and the 30-year Treasury yield has hit its highest level since 2007. J.P. Morgan's Phil Camporeale summed it up: "the combination of slower-than-expected supply chain normalization and market questioning of inflation-fighting credibility has lowered the bar for a rate hike."

This marks a sharp reversal from 2025, when the Fed cut rates in September, October, and December; FOMC projections have shifted decisively toward hikes since mid-2026. It's one of the clearest cases yet of a foreign conflict directly overriding a central bank's domestic-driven policy path.

**Why it matters:** A hike would raise borrowing costs for mortgages, credit cards, and business loans just as affordability concerns already weigh on consumer sentiment — and on Trump's approval numbers, below. It's also an unusual admission that a foreign war, not domestic demand, is now the dominant driver of US inflation. Watch the next two CPI prints and any further Gulf escalation — both were flagged as the deciding factors for the September call.

## Ukraine's Parliament Blocks Reform Bill, Putting €3.7B in EU Aid at Risk

**Ukraine's parliament fell 30 votes short of passing a law eliminating tax exemptions on small postal packages on September 1 — one of 12 conditions tied to €3.7 billion in EU Macro-Financial Assistance the European Commission says is now at risk.**

The blocked bill is one of 44 reforms the government has set a November 1 deadline to implement; further related bills are scheduled for debate September 2–3. EU Commission spokesperson Balazs Ujvari called it "a very important law because it plays an important role in mobilizing revenue for the Ukrainian budget." Zelensky addressed the delay directly: "they may be difficult decisions. They may be unpleasant decisions. They may be unpopular decisions. But they are necessary."

This isn't a one-off — Ukraine already missed €3.7 billion in EU support in 2025 under the same mechanism, and the Commission warned on August 25 that continued rule-of-law progress is "vital" for EU membership, just days after parliament blocked five earlier anti-corruption bills. The repeat pattern is what's turned this from a single legislative fight into something Brussels is now tracking closely.

**Why it matters:** Reform stalls, funding withheld, officials warn it undermines the war effort — the recurrence signals a structural risk to Ukraine's financing, not a one-off fight, and complicates Kyiv's EU accession case just as Western attention is split by the Iran war. For ordinary Ukrainians, delayed EU funds mean delayed wartime budget support. Watch the September 2–3 votes for whether parliament reverses course before November 1.

## Trump's Approval Ties Career Low at 33% as Army Secretary Quits Amid Pentagon Feud

**Trump's approval rating has settled at 33% in the latest Reuters/Ipsos survey — tying the lowest point of his political career, the third time that poll has landed there — while Army Secretary Dan Driscoll resigned Monday after months of friction with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth.**

The 33% figure is a 14-point drop from the 47% Trump held at the start of his second term; disapproval sits at 63%, with 71% specifically disapproving of his cost-of-living handling. Pollsters tied it to the unpopular Iran war and rising prices, and Reuters/Ipsos found Democrats "more fired up" for the midterms as a result. Driscoll's resignation followed Hegseth ousting Gen. Randy George, a close Driscoll ally, as Army Chief of Staff in April, and Gen. Chris Donahue's departure — both had backed an Army drone battalion built on Ukraine-war lessons that Hegseth has since moved to phase out. Driscoll, an Iraq War veteran confirmed to the role in February 2025, had reportedly been personally asked by Trump to stay through the midterms before deciding to leave anyway.

Both stories land against a midterm backdrop where election administration is contested too: Tarrant County, Texas has approved cutting polling locations by 92 versus 2022, and a Trump executive order on rapid mail-ballot vetting remains tied up in the courts. Deputy Army Undersecretary Dave Fitzgerald is also expected to step down this week.

**Why it matters:** A president's approval stuck in the low-to-mid 30s heading into a midterm year has historically preceded major losses for the incumbent's party, and a visible Pentagon leadership fight adds to that picture right as national-security credibility matters most given the Iran war. The fight itself — over a unit built on Ukraine-war lessons — is a telling signal of Hegseth's Pentagon prioritizing loyalty over battlefield-derived doctrine. Watch whether Fitzgerald's exit is followed by more departures, and whether affordability numbers move once the Fed's rate decision lands.

## UK's New PM Burnham Sets Out Devolution-Focused Opening Agenda

**Prime Minister Andy Burnham gave his first statement to the House of Commons on September 1, centering his agenda on devolving power to England's regions and cutting costs on energy bills, pub rates, and bus fares — with a full fiscal plan due in October.**

Burnham, who became the UK's seventh prime minister in a decade after succeeding Keir Starmer in July, announced "Number 10 North," a unit driving regional growth strategy, and extended devolution offers to every English area without existing devolved powers, plus Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. Near-term measures include a VAT cut on electricity bills, reduced business rates for pubs and live-music venues, and reinstating the £2 bus fare cap through 2027 — funded, the government says, by "reprioritising within government." He also reversed a planned early-release scheme for some offenses, stating "anyone convicted of manslaughter, death by dangerous driving or indecent assault will not now see any change."

The devolution-first framing follows a bruising transition: Starmer had warned that Burnham's instincts — including wanting to get "beyond this thing of being in hock to the bond markets" — risked a Liz Truss-style reaction. UK gilt yields, which sold off during the leadership uncertainty, have since steadied. Burnham reaffirmed "steadfast support" for Ukraine and confirmed he'll attend COP31 in Turkey this November.

**Why it matters:** October's budget statement is the real test of whether Burnham's devolution and cost-relief agenda is fiscally credible to bond markets that were already jittery during his rise to power — a repeat of 2022's mini-budget crisis is the scenario everyone's watching for. His early moves suggest a government banking visible cost-of-living wins before that harder fiscal reckoning arrives. How markets react in October will likely determine whether Burnham can govern on his own terms.

## China Steps Up Preparations for a Taiwan Blockade, Taipei Warns

**Taiwan's defense ministry says the PLA is intensifying preparations for a blockade of the island, according to its 2026 China Military Power Report submitted to the legislature this week alongside a five-year force development plan and defense budget request.**

The ministry's assessment says PLA exercises increasingly focus on "joint blockade and control" of Taiwan's air and sea routes — maritime and air blockades, joint firepower strikes, and amphibious landing drills — aimed at pressuring Taiwan and denying access to any foreign forces that might intervene. Taiwan says it's developing countermeasures, having tested them for the first time during an August 10-day war-games exercise. Beijing continues to describe Taiwan as part of China and hasn't ruled out force, and the report was submitted alongside Taiwan's own proposed defense budget.

The report lands the same week Xi Jinping appeared alongside Russia and Iran at the Bishkek SCO summit, underscoring Beijing positioning itself within a bloc of governments contesting US-led pressure campaigns, even while avoiding direct involvement in the Iran conflict itself.

**Why it matters:** A confirmed shift in PLA training toward blockade scenarios — not just show-of-force exercises — is the kind of change analysts treat as a leading indicator of contingency planning. Paired with China's presence at a summit rallying support for Iran, it suggests Beijing is managing friction with Washington on multiple fronts at once. Watch the actual Taiwanese defense budget figures when finalized — that's where the seriousness of Taipei's response will show up concretely.

### 🔍 The Big Picture

Nearly every story today traces back to the same fault line: the Iran war has stopped being a regional conflict and become the organizing event for global politics and economics. It's forcing the Fed's hand on rates, pushing Russia, Iran, and China into closer alignment at forums like the SCO even as India visibly resists that framing, and testing whether the Gulf shipping lanes the world economy depends on can keep functioning under repeated strikes. At the same time, two of the West's key governments — the UK under a brand-new PM and Ukraine mid-reform — are navigating their own separate credibility tests with bond markets and Brussels, tests that would be hard enough without a live war reshaping the backdrop.

The thread connecting Washington's sinking approval numbers, Beijing's Taiwan posture, and the Bishkek summit is a world where US pressure campaigns — sanctions on Iran, support for Ukraine, deterrence toward China — are increasingly met with coordinated pushback rather than isolated resistance, even if that pushback (see Modi's comments) isn't as unified as its participants might like to project. Watch whether the September Fed decision and the next Hormuz escalation cycle move in tandem — that link, more than any single headline, will define how contained or how costly this period turns out to be.
